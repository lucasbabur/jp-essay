# Style

Write in classic style. The writer has seen something true and points the reader at it, the way you would show a friend something through a window. The reader is smart, attentive and new to this, an equal in conversation rather than a student. Everything below follows from that picture.

**Say something.** Each paragraph makes one claim the reader could disagree with, and the essay's verdicts are stated at the strength the evidence supports. A vague claim is easy to keep true and worthless to read, so make it bold, then check it is still true. Qualify a claim only where there is a real doubt, and name the doubt ("the study followed 96 people, all volunteers").

**Write to the reader's question.** The reader came with a question about their own life, so open on their situation and keep returning to it; the essay ends by telling them what to do or believe. A study earns its place by moving that answer, and a detail about a study that the reader's decision does not turn on stays in the notes. A scene the reader is plainly asked to imagine ("Picture two neighbours") is not a fact and needs no source.

**Carry it on one case.** A reader follows a person further than an argument. Put the carrier, someone in the reader's situation or a real case from the notes, on stage in the first paragraph, and bring them back whenever the argument turns, so the evidence happens to someone. A story that carries no claim is decoration, and decoration costs understanding.

**Keep the question open.** Open on a gap between what the reader believes and what turns out to be true, and close it at the end. Announcing the verdict in the first paragraph tells the reader the puzzle is solved, and readers stop for solved puzzles. A question mark is optional; the gap is not.

**Start from the thing itself.** Put the scene, the person, the number or the case before the idea it shows, and let the reader see the idea arrive. Every paragraph stands on at least one specific from `sources.md`. When a paragraph has no specific, it is either padding or a sign the material is thin; cut it or say plainly that it is reasoning.

**Use the literal word, and a true comparison.** Prefer the literal phrase, but a comparison that fits and is true earns its place, because it is often the line the reader carries away; carry one well rather than scattering several. Prefer common words and concrete nouns, and put actions in verbs ("they decided", not "they made a decision").

**One name per thing.** Once you call something a "rule", keep calling it a rule; a new word makes the reader think you mean a new thing. Give each sentence one main idea, with the actor as the subject when the actor matters.

**Explain what this reader lacks.** The `Reader:` line says what the reader already knows. Explain what falls outside it, once, where it first matters, and preferably by example inside the sentence's own flow. Leave alone what any educated reader knows. Defining every term buries the argument as surely as defining none.

**Let rhythm follow the thought.** Most sentences run 12 to 30 words. A short sentence lands hardest after longer ones, so save it for a point that deserves the weight. A long sentence is fine when its parts stay in order; split it when the reader would have to read it twice.

**Move the argument with logic words.** Connect each paragraph to the one before through the reasoning (but, so, because, which means, yet), and open each paragraph with a sentence that does that work.

**One study per paragraph.** Name at most one study in a paragraph, and keep the numbers that make the claim concrete; a reader trusts a figure more than an adjective. A reader follows a person or a finding; a queue of studies with sample sizes and caveats reads as a literature review. What a curious reader might want to check goes in a short footnote; the rest stays in the notes.

**Quote when the words beat a paraphrase.** A person's own phrasing is evidence and voice at once, so quote it verbatim from the notes and keep it short. Name a researcher or source in the sentence when the name helps the reader trust or follow the story; otherwise state the finding.

**Stop when the question is answered.** End on the answer to the reader's question, what to do or believe and when, carried by a consequence or a scene rather than stated as a moral. The reader has just read the essay and needs no summary of it, and a moral tacked on at the end sounds like a speech.

**Rule zero.** Break any of these sooner than write something false, stiff or unclear.
